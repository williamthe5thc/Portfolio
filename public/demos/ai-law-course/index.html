AI & Law Design Record

Graduate capstone · S.J. Quinney College of Law, University of Utah · Spring 2025

What this page is. The course itself was built and delivered in Canvas and is not publicly accessible, so this is a record of the design work rather than a copy of the course. It was designed and built by a three-person capstone team; I did most of the Canvas construction. Content reflects the AI landscape as of early 2025. The models have moved on since; this page documents the instructional design decisions.

The course

Ten modules designed for Master of Legal Studies students, to run online and asynchronously over a 13-week semester. The course was built in Canvas but not taught while I was in the program. Learners would arrive with legal backgrounds and no technical training, so the sequence moves from what the tools are, through how to direct them, into the professional and ethical questions that come with using them in practice.

The syllabus plans sixteen graded pieces across the semester. Four-fifths of the grade goes to applied work, because a legal audience needs to use these tools, not only describe them.

How it was designed: read the documents

These are the actual working documents, converted to web pages. Names and contact details of the faculty sponsor and teammates have been removed.

  • Client interview: the scoping conversation with the faculty sponsor. His steer shaped the whole build: teach strategies, not platforms, and do not make learners create a dozen accounts.
  • HCI cognitive walkthrough: a usability walkthrough of our own course, working from a persona of a part-time MLS student with medium-to-low technical confidence. Together with a think-aloud session and a usability survey, it turned up problems in specific parts of the prototype and led to four high-priority recommendations for the revision.
  • Instructional strategies: the approach chosen for each part of the course, with the reasoning written down.
  • Media selection: why each medium suited each kind of content.

Also part of the process but not published here: the syllabus, the team collaboration plan, and the stakeholder review, all of which carry personal contact details.

What I would change

The AI content dated faster than the design did. Anything naming specific model capabilities needed revision within months, while the module sequence, the assessment mix, and the scaffolding held up. If I rebuilt it, I would separate volatile tool-specific material from the durable conceptual spine so the course could be updated without redesigning it.
