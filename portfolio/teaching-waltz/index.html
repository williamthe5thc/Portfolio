Teaching the Waltz Online Course

A classmate and I designed a self-paced Canvas course that teaches complete beginners to dance the waltz. I filmed the lessons with a dancer, showing common mistakes next to the correct form, and we tested the course with a small group of four learners.

The complete formative evaluation report, including the assessment design.

View document (PDF)

My role: Co-designer with a classmate. I handled the video and our subject matter expert: I coordinated with the dancer who taught in our videos, filmed the lessons with him, turned the footage into course videos with transcripts and optional captions, and proofed the finished course. My classmate built the Canvas pages, assessments and rubrics.

  • 4 Participants in small-group formative evaluation
  • 2 Modules tested

The problem

The course was for adults who want to learn the waltz but don't have the time or access for in-person classes. They are complete beginners, may not have a partner, and have no instructor there to correct their form. The project was also our chance to run the whole instructional design process, from course goals through evaluation. Our own constraint was the end-of-semester deadline.

  • Teaching a physical, partnered skill through a self-paced online course, with no instructor in the room to correct form
  • Showing learners what common mistakes look like, and asking them to spot those mistakes
  • Including learners who don't have a partner, or who can't dance themselves
  • Keeping learners part of a dancing community while they study on their own

What I found

The gap was the skill itself: beginners who can't yet dance the box step, the progressive basic or the underarm turn. The two course outcomes follow from that: perform the basic steps, and explain the waltz's historical, social and physical context. The needs assessment also named motivation as a cause, since ballroom dancing isn't a skill most people need, so the design builds in contact with other learners and regular feedback from the instructor.

What I did

  • Lesson videos alongside written lead and follow instructions, so learners can use whichever helps them
  • Videos filmed for the course that show common mistakes next to the correct form
  • Discussions where learners share challenges and successes and reply to each other
  • A video submission graded by rubric, with an alternative for learners who can't dance

What happened

  • Tested with a small group of four participants, who agreed the modules were well organized, clear and easy to get to. View the evaluation report
  • Interviews found the assessments matched the learning objectives and the grading criteria were clear
  • Every participant said they wanted more video, especially of the whole dance
  • The results led to a revision plan for the videos, captions, diagrams and wording

What I'd change

Next time I would give the dancer a script, so what he says in the videos lines up with the assignments, and film more of the dance with a partner. I would also record somewhere quiet: background noise was the one problem we chose not to fix, because it meant re-filming.

About this Project

Graduate final project at the University of Utah, co-designed with a classmate. The course has two modules. The first teaches the box step, the progressive basic and the underarm turn; the second covers the dance's history, its health benefits and its place in social life. Dance is learned by doing, and an online course can't correct anyone in the moment, so the design plans for mistakes instead: the lesson videos show common errors next to the correct form, and learners send in a video of their own dancing for instructor feedback.

ADDIE Methodology

Analysis

Needs Assessment: We worked from a written needs assessment. The evidence for the need came from my own experience: at dances, the people I asked to dance often didn't know the waltz, and I ended up teaching them. No survey of prospective learners was run.

Learner Analysis: Able-bodied adults of any age and background who want to learn a ballroom dance, with little or no dance experience, a computer and an internet connection. They can watch, read or listen to instruction. We wrote personas for them and kept referring back to them while designing.

Context Analysis: A self-paced course in Canvas. Learners practice at home, alone or with a partner, and send in video for feedback.

Design

Instructional Strategy: Module 1 builds from the box step to the progressive basic to the underarm turn with a partner, and ends with a video submission. Each step has a video and written instructions for the lead and the follow. The motivation plan follows the ARCS model: physical activity and varied lessons for attention, dancing in real settings for relevance, instructor feedback for confidence, and clear rubrics for satisfaction.

Assessment Strategy: Module 1 has a video of the learner dancing all three steps, graded by rubric with instructor feedback; a Common Mistakes page with a video of typical errors (the plan's graded assignment to spot mistakes in a dance video wasn't built); a five-question quiz with two attempts and a 15-minute limit, where each wrong answer links to the page to review; and a discussion on challenges and successes. Module 2 has a 500 to 1,000 word essay on the history of the waltz, a concept map on the health benefits of dancing, and a discussion where learners share a waltz from a film, a piece of music or social life. Discussions and the concept map count for 30% of the grade, and the video, essay and quiz for 70%.

Universal Design: Captions and transcripts on every video, written instructions next to the videos, alt text on images, sans-serif type, good contrast and no tables. Learners who can't dance can instead film themselves talking another person through the steps.

Development

Content Creation: I filmed a dancer, a friend who agreed to be our instructor, instead of using existing footage, so the videos could show common mistakes next to the correct form. I cut the footage into a separate video for each step and one for the mistakes, and wrote a transcript for each.

Implementation

Pilot Testing: We tested both modules with a small group of four participants, using a feedback survey and interviews.

Evaluation

Formative Assessment: Participants took 5 to 30 minutes per module, with averages of 10.75 minutes for the first and 14.75 for the second. They felt at least somewhat competent afterwards. Opinions were split on whether there were enough visual aids, and the common suggestion was more step diagrams.

Continuous Improvement: The revisions we planned from the results: fix the title slides on the common-mistakes video, correct the captions and let learners turn them off, split the videos into lead and follow, add diagrams for the progressive basic and the underarm turn, and use "lead" and "follow" throughout instead of gendered terms. We decided not to re-film for better audio, because it didn't get in the way of understanding.
