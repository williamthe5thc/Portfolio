Graduate Curriculum Design Project - AI & Law Course

A three-person graduate capstone team designed and built this ten-module AI and Law course in Canvas for Master of Legal Studies students. We ran a client interview with the faculty sponsor, developed personas, ran a cognitive walkthrough on our own design, and validated it with a stakeholder review before handover. The course was not taught while I was in the program.

The course lives in Canvas and cannot be linked publicly, so this opens the design record instead.

View design record

My role: One of three designers on a graduate capstone team. I did most of the Canvas build and wrote the assessment design for the advanced prompting week.

  • 10 Graduate modules designed
  • 16 Graded pieces in the course syllabus

The problem

The faculty sponsor taught a course on AI and law and wanted an online, asynchronous course in Canvas for Master of Legal Studies students. The learners are legal professionals without technical training. At the client interview the sponsor's brief was plain: keep it simple and not too technical, teach strategies rather than platforms, use cheap or free tools, and don't make learners create accounts on a long list of AI sites.

  • Learners with legal backgrounds and no technical training, who would lose interest if the course went too deep
  • Every new AI tool meant another account to create, so the course had to lean on strategies more than tools
  • AI tools change quickly, so anything tied to one platform would date fast
  • Online and asynchronous, so every instruction had to work without a live class

What I did

  • Sequenced prompting from basic (Module 2) to advanced (Module 6), then applied it to ethics (Module 7) and legal tools (Modules 5 and 8)
  • In my assessment plan for the advanced prompting week, an AI use policy that asks learners to name the tool and submit their prompt, instead of banning AI
  • A syllabus with low-stakes quizzes about every other week and the lowest score dropped, so most of the grade (80%) rests on applied work: assignments, a midterm project and a final portfolio
  • A final portfolio learners build across the semester, with assignments in Modules 4, 7, 8 and 9 feeding into it
  • I set up a test module in Canvas to try embeds (video, iFrames, JavaScript, an AI chatbot) before adding them to the course
  • Checked the content and design with the faculty sponsor, from the client interview through a stakeholder review before handover

What happened

  • Built ten modules in Canvas for an online, asynchronous MLS course. It had not been taught by the time I finished the program
  • Tested the prototype with a cognitive walkthrough, a think-aloud session and a 10-question usability survey, then built four high-priority recommendations into the revision plan
  • Wrote the assessment design for the advanced prompting week myself: specifications by cognitive level, rubrics and item analysis (see the work below)
  • At the final stakeholder review, the faculty sponsor said the course covered the learning goals and liked the layout. Their suggestions: fewer overlapping quizzes, more scholarly readings, quicker feedback than essays allow, and a more readable banner font

What I'd change

The AI content dated faster than the design did. Anything naming a specific model or tool needed revising within months, while the module sequence and the assessment mix held up. Next time I would keep tool-specific material separate from the core lessons so it can be swapped without redesigning the course, and I would cut the overlap the sponsor pointed out between quizzes and matching activities.

About this Project

A graduate capstone, built by a team of three for the University of Utah's S.J. Quinney College of Law. The job was to teach artificial intelligence to Master of Legal Studies students without technical backgrounds, working with a faculty sponsor who was both our client and our subject matter expert, and reviewed by a two-person capstone committee. We used a competency-based design to build ten modules for a 13-week online course, covering AI foundations, prompt engineering, legal research, media, and professional ethics. The syllabus assesses learners through five quizzes, five discussions, four assignments, a midterm project and a final project. The course was built in Canvas but not taught while I was in the program.
